What does one rotation cost?

Instructions

The logical compilation layers in CUDA-Q are P0 (logical circuit), P1 (logical placement), P2 (error-correcting encoding), P3 (physical connectivity and schedule), and P4 (control signals). This widget follows that notation and stops at P3. New to the layers? Start with the introductory logical-compilation widget, which walks a full circuit through P0 to P4; this widget assumes that background and follows one rotation in depth.

Every T gate in a fault-tolerant circuit consumes one high-quality T state (a magic state). A T-state factory distills many noisy raw T states into fewer, better ones. This widget follows a single rotation gate through that pipeline.

  1. P0 · Circuit: choose the error target for the whole rotation, then a synthesis tolerance ε. Read the Clifford+T gate sequence and note how many T gates it contains.
  2. P1 · Regions: see where operations run and where T states are built.
  3. P2 · Encoding: set the surface-code distance and pick a distillation protocol.
  4. P3 · Noise and schedule: vary the raw T-state error, then compare the error bound, physical qubits, and scheduled duration of all four factories in the summary.
  5. Solve the design challenge in the Summary, then take the AI challenge at the end: use “View your CUDA-Q code” as a starting point to budget a small algorithm of your own.

All 32 configurations were precompiled using the logical capabilities in CUDA-Q. Error bounds come from exact fault enumeration of independent raw-state errors. Results are model estimates, not hardware measurements.

Trade-offs: qubits and time are two separate constraints, and the choices along the way trade one for the other. A bigger factory delivers T states faster but reserves more qubits; a leaner one saves qubits and makes the computation wait. This widget helps you find where those trade-offs lie.

P0 Synthesize the logical circuit

⌄

The logical circuit begins with one qubit prepared in |0⟩ and rotated by Rᵧ(0.37). Fault-tolerant hardware cannot apply an arbitrary rotation directly, so the compiler synthesizes it as a sequence of H, S, and T gates. First fix the error target: that is the whole budget for this rotation. Then pick a synthesis tolerance ε; a tighter ε needs more T gates and uses less of the budget, and every T gate will later cost a T state.

P1 Place the circuit and factory in regions

⌄

The logical machine has two regions. The T-state factory is its own region, so it runs in parallel with compute: it can prepare T states ahead of time, ready whenever the circuit reaches a T gate.

T states must be transferred from the factory to compute. On real hardware that costs time and qubits; the overhead is not counted in this exercise.

P2 Choose the encoding and factory protocol

⌄

Both regions use the rotated surface code. The code distance d sets how many physical qubits each encoded patch reserves. The factory’s distillation protocol sets how many raw T states it consumes, how clean its outputs are, and how many patches it needs. Distillation works by combining many noisy T states through a Clifford circuit and measuring checks that would reveal an error; a batch whose checks all pass is kept, and the rest are thrown away.

P3 Set physical noise and read the schedule

⌄

Connectivity and timing stay fixed here: 1 µs per surface-code cycle, d cycles per encoded operation, and 10 µs classical reaction time. The one physical input you control is how noisy the raw T states are before distillation.

The scheduled duration is the time for one accepting pass with the factory and compute running in parallel. It matters because the factory sets the pace: the compute patch can only apply a T gate when a T state is ready, so a slow factory leaves it idle, and in a real algorithm that wait repeats for every rotation. Qubits and time are the two sides of the bill, and the factory comparison in the summary shows how one can be traded for the other.
